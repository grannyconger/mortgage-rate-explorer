    History inside this window

    Why a percent matters

    A rate is just a number until you see it as a monthly bill. Same house, same loan — only the rate changes.

    Every payment below is for a 30-year loan and covers only principal and interest — not property taxes or insurance.

    Words you will meet on this page

    Click any term to open its explanation.

    Mortgage rate

    The yearly interest percentage a lender charges on a home loan. At 7%, a $300,000 loan costs roughly $21,000 in interest during its first year alone. Lower is cheaper for the borrower.

    Fixed rate

    The rate is locked for the whole life of the loan. A 30-year fixed at 6% is still 6% in year 29. That predictability is why it is the most popular home loan in America.

    30-year vs 15-year

    Two lengths of the same idea. The 15-year is paid off in half the time, which means a higher monthly payment but a lower rate and far less total interest. Lenders charge less because they get their money back sooner and carry less risk.

    5/1 ARM

    An Adjustable-Rate Mortgage. The rate is fixed for the first 5 years, then resets once a year after that. It usually starts cheaper than a fixed loan, but the borrower carries the risk that rates rise later. Freddie Mac stopped publishing this series in November 2022, which is why its line simply stops.

    Basis point

    One hundredth of a percent. A move from 6.50% to 6.75% is “25 basis points”. Professionals use the term because saying “a quarter of a percentage point” all day is clumsy.

    Moving average

    Instead of plotting one week on its own, you plot the average of the last several weeks. Small random bumps cancel out and the real direction shows through. That is exactly what the Smoothing control does.

    PMMS (the survey)

    The Primary Mortgage Market Survey. Freddie Mac asks lenders across the country what rate they are offering a well-qualified borrower, then averages the answers. It is a national average, so a real quote you receive will differ.

    Outlier

    A data point sitting far away from all the others. The 18.63% week in October 1981 is the famous outlier here — and unlike most outliers, it is completely real.

    Correlation is not causation

    Two lines moving together does not prove one caused the other. Mortgage rates track the wider economy, so many things rise and fall alongside them without being the reason.
